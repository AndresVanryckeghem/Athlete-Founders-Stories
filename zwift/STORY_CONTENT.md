# Zwift — Final Story Content
# Source: 7 transcripts (King of the Ride, Sigma Sports Unplugged, CyclingTips From the Top, Rouleur, Iron Foundry, My Other Passion, Overnight Success)
# Founder: Eric Min (co-founders: Alaric Marin, Jon Mayfield, Scott Barger)

---

## Chapter 1 — "The Kid Who Couldn't Recover"
**Short label:** The Racer

1. `#` Eric Min was born in Seoul, South Korea. His family moved to the US in the 1970s, settled in New Jersey, then the Bronx. He grew up a New Yorker.
2. He found cycling at 14, through a friend named Travis Moomin who got into touring with the American Youth Hostel. Before a driver's license, a bike was freedom. Eric insisted on buying the same 56 centimeter frame as his taller friend. He rides a 49 today.
3. He joined the Sleepy Hollow Bicycle Club in North Tarrytown, New York. Three brothers, triplets named Whalen, ran the club. They taught him everything about training, took him to races, and introduced him to the sport that would define his life.
4. He traveled the country doing junior races. Went to the Olympic Training Center to get tested. The result was clear. **'I just didn't have the natural talent that allowed me to recover day after day. I'd be good for one day, but then the second, third day, my performance would decline.'**
5. He calls it the best thing that happened to him. **'I'm glad I found that early on, because I think I could have just lied to myself about what I could be.'**

## Chapter 2 — "Wall Street, Then Walking Away"
**Short label:** JP Morgan

1. `#` His girlfriend, now wife, got him on track. She was an intern at JP Morgan and told him to apply. He didn't have the grades or credentials, but someone liked him. He got in on the technology side and spent eight years building trading systems for commodities.
2. He quit cycling to focus on his career. **'I was never very good at doing many things at the same time. I have to stay focused on one thing and just give everything to that one thing.'**
3. After eight years, he asked himself one question: do I want to be my boss? The answer was obvious. **'I didn't want to have his responsibility. And I thought my path forward was to be an independent.'**
4. He was 28. No children. No mortgage. **'If I'm ever going to do something entrepreneurial, it's now. It's not 10 years from now.'**

## Chapter 3 — "The First Startup That Paid for the Second"
**Short label:** Sakonnet

1. `#` Eric and his partner Alaric Marin left JP Morgan and started Sakonnet Technology in 1998, building cloud based trading systems for oil and gas before the word cloud even existed. They called it Application Hosting Services.
2. They raised about $600,000 from friends and family on a 50 page business plan nobody fully understood. **'My brother invested $100,000 very early on. He didn't understand what we were doing. He thought, I'll never see that money again.'**
3. Their biggest client was a German energy company called EON. They ate off that one client for over a decade. **'If you lose that one client, the business is dead.'**
4. The business took Eric to London. He stayed for 18 years. Sakonnet turned a profit and returned money to its investors. That track record became the credibility Eric would later use to raise capital for Zwift.
5. On the side, they also sponsored a small U23 cycling team called Sakonnet. It produced three or four professional riders, including Phil Gaimon. The cycling world never fully left him.

## Chapter 4 — "Missing Central Park"
**Short label:** The Problem

1. `#` In New York, Eric had lived two blocks from Central Park. A 10 kilometer circuit he could reach in two minutes. Training, competition, community, social rides, all of it happened there.
2. When he moved to London, he lost every piece of that. Richmond Park was 30 minutes away. The weather was bad. Traffic was everywhere. **'I resorted to indoor riding.'**
3. He bought every indoor cycling product on the market. Not because he wanted to start a business. Because he needed something to keep him engaged. CompuTrainer had a 3D simulator that never delivered. Most products were solo experiences with bad graphics.
4. Then he found a product called Tour de Giro, built by two former IBM consultants. The graphics were terrible. The UI was terrible. But he raced in it with about 10 other people. **'At the end of that race, I felt like I was there with these competitors. And I was like, this could work.'**
5. That feeling, being with other people while riding indoors, was the entire spark. **'If we could just make the whole experience better, I thought there was something there.'**

## Chapter 5 — "The Last Thing He Wanted to Do"
**Short label:** Choosing Cycling

1. `#` After exiting Sakonnet, Eric started looking for his next venture. He explored education, healthcare, hospitality. He even looked at making it easier to rent bikes at hotels. The math never worked.
2. The last industry he wanted to touch was cycling. **'I probably should not invest in my passion, because you could easily justify it. I know a lot of people who own bike shops. It's a passion play. And actually, it's very bad business.'**
3. His brother told him plainly: **'What are you doing? You don't know anything about that space.'** Eric stopped. **'What do I know? I know finance and I know cycling.'**
4. He had a checklist: internet enabled, global, delivered as a service, subscription model. A consumer business, not B2B, because he never wanted to depend on one client again. Indoor cycling checked every box.
5. His partner Alaric, who is not a cyclist, was convinced by a different argument entirely. **'If we can start a business where we're inspiring people to be active, to be fit, to be healthy, who wouldn't want to back a business like that?'**

## Chapter 6 — "The Guy Coding at 2 AM with a Newborn"
**Short label:** Finding Jon

1. `#` In February 2010, a video game programmer named Jon Mayfield discovered a 30 mile bike path 100 meters behind his new house in Southern California. By his third ride, he was hooked.
2. Then daylight savings hit. He had a newborn. His wife was running out of patience with how much time he spent riding. He bought a dumb trainer, pedaled for five minutes, and got off. **'This is horrible.'**
3. Jon worked in the video game industry, building PlayStation and Nintendo 64 games. So he did what a game developer does: he started building his own solution. He glued wires to his bike, wrote firmware to read his wheel speed, converted it to watts, and displayed them on screen.
4. All of this happened between 10 PM and 4 AM while he was on night duty with the baby. **'Half the time the kid is either down in the crib or up in some sort of chair next to me while I'm coding at 2 AM and hopping on a bike that's going nowhere.'**
5. By December 2010, he had a 3D bicycle on a road with power data. By January 2011, he had built a steering controller that went under the front tire. By spring 2012, he posted what he'd built on the Slow Twitch triathlon forum. The response was more positive than he expected.
6. He got four or five emails from entrepreneurs wanting to partner. None of them felt right. **'It seemed like nobody really serious, or I didn't like the tone, or it was kind of like you do all the work and then I'll... I just got a bad vibe.'**

## Chapter 7 — "Knocking on Jon's Door"
**Short label:** The Partnership

1. `#` In November 2013, Eric Googled for indoor cycling games and Jon Mayfield's project was the first result. He saw bad graphics and a single user experience. But he also saw the beginnings of something.
2. Other people had emailed Jon. Eric was the only one who actually showed up. **'I flew from London to LA and knocked on his front door to meet him in person and get a demo and go for a ride.'**
3. Jon was nervous. He had dressed in cycling clothes to demo the software. His heart rate strap was on. When he hopped off the bike to point at the screen, his heart rate read 160. It was displayed live for Eric and Scott Barger to see. **'I'm like trying to switch the screens over because it seems a little bit embarrassing.'**
4. They rode Mount Baldy together. A week later, Eric called and said: let's form a company. Jon was skeptical. **'When he told me he thought we could have 50,000 users paying for the product someday, I was like, eh, probably not.'**
5. Eric offered a safety net: if the company shut down within 12 months, the contract guaranteed Jon's salary. **'He knew that was never going to be a clause that got activated, and he convinced me.'** Within two weeks, Jon quit his job.

## Chapter 8 — "A 5K Island and Three Jerseys"
**Short label:** Building the MVP

1. `#` The company was created in January 2014. Four co founders: Eric, Alaric, Jon, and Scott Barger. Jon left his old job on a Friday at 6 PM and drove to an Airbnb where the others were waiting. His commute between jobs was one hour.
2. They spent the first weekend putting sticky notes on a wall. **'What about cycling gyms? What about running? What about whatever?'** Jon's first task the following week was to convert his prototype into a running product using a Garmin foot pod. He ran around his neighborhood with a tablet in front of his face. They agreed: cycling was the right sport.
3. They set up an office in Long Beach, hired a team of about eight, and spent six months building an MVP. Eric and Alaric funded the seed themselves, roughly a million dollars each.
4. The beta launched in October 2014 with a single island called Jarvis, a real island in the South Pacific chosen because it had no Strava segments. The map was only **5 kilometers**. **'You want people to see each other. If you have a big map and they don't see each other, it doesn't feel social.'**
5. There were exactly three rewards: a KOM jersey, a sprint jersey, and a fastest lap jersey. Each reset every hour. That was the entire product. No events, no racing, no training mode. **'People showed up and loved chasing these things.'**

## Chapter 9 — "200 Friends, 100 Checks"
**Short label:** Raising Capital

1. `#` Eric told his co founders the company was worth $20 million. They thought he was on drugs. **'I just came up with that number.'**
2. He pitched to 200 friends, family, and friends of friends. 100 of them invested. The plan was to raise $3 million. They raised **$7 million**. **'Once you have some momentum with investors, it's very easy to convince others. So I created this demand. Some of it is artificial.'**
3. Many of those investors had made money from Sakonnet and simply rolled it into Zwift. They believed in Eric even when they didn't fully understand the product.
4. That $7 million lasted about 15 months. Eric went back on the road and raised another $10 million at a **$100 million valuation**. Later came a Series C of **$450 million**. Total capital raised: roughly half a billion dollars.
5. The advice he now gives first time entrepreneurs: **'It takes five years to validate a business. It takes 10 years to build a business. Do you have the stomach for that?'**

## Chapter 10 — "The Strava Growth Hack"
**Short label:** Getting to Market

1. `#` The beta launch was simultaneous at Rapha stores in London, New York, and San Francisco, with Team Sky riders, Pinarello bikes, Wahoo trainers, and media in all three locations. **'Looking back, I don't know what the hell I was thinking. But that's how ambitious we were.'**
2. They hired a PR firm before launch, not after. **'One of the smart things we did in the beginning was to hire a PR firm. Having all these brands on our platform gave it authenticity.'**
3. 13,000 people signed up for the beta in the first week. But the real growth hack was simpler: Strava integration from day one. When riders uploaded from Jarvis Island, their Strava followers saw rides appearing in the South Pacific. **'People asked me, I didn't know you were in the South Pacific. This is how people started to hear about Zwift.'**
4. Ted King became their first ambassador. The community itself became the support team. **'It wasn't us having to do all the customer support. It was Facebook groups from passionate Zwifters helping figure out what you need.'**

## Chapter 11 — "Consumers Are Extremely Lazy"
**Short label:** Friction

1. `#` The barrier to entry was enormous. You needed a laptop, an ANT Plus dongle, a specific graphics card, a Wi Fi connection in your basement, and a compatible smart trainer. **'The barrier to entry is more of a business problem than a technical one.'**
2. Jon, the natural pessimist, doubted anyone would jump through that many hoops. **'Are there really 50,000 people who want to hook up all these wires and figure out all the stuff they gotta buy just to pedal this bike?'**
3. It turned out people would, because being with other riders was worth the hassle. But the friction never stopped being the main thing holding growth back.
4. Eric's central obsession, stated across multiple interviews over a decade: **'Consumers are extremely lazy. If you give them three clicks to do, they may not buy something. You've got to get rid of those clicks.'**
5. This is why Zwift eventually got into hardware. Not to compete with Wahoo or Tacx, but to simplify onboarding. Every $100 they could reduce the cost of entry opened up the market further. **'If you're new to indoor cycling, you shouldn't have to spend more than $500.'**

## Chapter 12 — "COVID Doubled the Business"
**Short label:** The Pandemic

1. `#` Zwift had invested heavily in infrastructure before the pandemic, preparing for a growth spike they expected to come gradually. COVID delivered it overnight. The business doubled.
2. **'We saw a huge rush of people coming in. These are people who wouldn't have considered indoor cycling, but were forced to because training was important. Being connected with one another was important.'**
3. That same period, ASO's Tour de France was about to be canceled. Eric told Yann LeMoyne, the CEO of ASO, that the Tour wasn't going to happen. Yann didn't believe him. **'It didn't happen.'**
4. Eric proposed a virtual Tour de France. But he added one condition: women's racing had to be included, with equal distance, equal prize money, and equal broadcast coverage. **'Knowing that it would be very difficult for them to not commit to doing a women's Tour de France going forward.'**
5. The virtual Tour de France was broadcast in over 100 countries. It led directly to the creation of the real **Tour de France Femmes avec Zwift**, now four years running. Eric was at every stage of the most recent edition. **'The crowds were insane. It was literally the men's Tour de France.'**

## Chapter 13 — "You're Just Taking on a Bigger Mortgage"
**Short label:** Unicorn

1. `#` Zwift raised its Series C, closing at $450 million. Reported valuation: over $1 billion. Eric's reaction when asked how that feels: **'We're just taking on a bigger mortgage.'**
2. He has never publicly confirmed the exact valuation. **'The company's worth way more than that, but we just won't talk about it.'**
3. By the most recent interviews, Zwift has roughly **300 to 350 employees**, with offices in Long Beach, London, and Rio de Janeiro. Eric himself moved from London to the Cotswolds during COVID, then to Santa Monica in 2022, to be closer to headquarters.
4. His proudest metric is not revenue or valuation. It's that people who Zwift regularly end up riding more outdoors, because they're fitter. **'If you're fit when you go outdoors, you have more fun. That's what brings people back.'**
5. **'We're still in the early years of building this business. If you want to really own the space, you have to invest long term and not focus too much on the short term things.'**
